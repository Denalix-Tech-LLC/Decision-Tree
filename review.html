TAS Decision-Tree Tool: A Review

An assessment of the EPA draft guiding Tribes on whether — and for which Clean Air Act sections — to seek “Treated as a State” status.

Source: ESE TAS tool decision tree.docx Draft rev. 11 Author Laura McKelvey ~2,236 words · 8 pp. Status Working draft

Bottom line

Strong, well-cited regulatory backbone and a genuinely useful “which sections fit my situation” map — but not yet publishable. The top fix: Decision-Tree Question 1 has its Yes/No branches inverted, so a Tribe answering honestly is steered toward the higher-risk path exactly where the stakes (litigation over jurisdictional boundaries) are highest. Second: the section titled “Decision Tree” contains no flowchart at all — only prose nested three-to-four levels deep.

01 What this document is

It’s an EPA guidance document that doubles as a self-service decision tool: it helps a federally recognized Tribe decide whether, and for which Clean Air Act (CAA) sections, to seek “Treated as a State” (TAS) status. Its own thesis is that a Tribe must “weigh the pros and cons… before making the decision,” and it addresses two audiences at once — Tribal air-program staff and Tribal leadership/Council. It runs Background → benefits → eligibility criteria → purpose → risk → a repeated criteria list → the “Decision Tree.”

02 The decision tree, corrected

The document names its centerpiece a “Decision Tree” but delivers deeply indented prose, and its first branch is logically backwards. Below is the intended logic, reconstructed and repaired — the artifact the title promises.

A Tribe is weighing TAS under the Clean Air Act
Question 1 · Boundaries Are any of the Tribe’s jurisdictional boundaries disputed?
NO — boundaries are clear

Lower risk. Coordinate with leadership & counsel, then move to Question 2 to choose which CAA sections to pursue.

YES — some or all disputed

Higher risk. An appropriate governmental entity may comment on, and after approval judicially challenge, the boundaries. Weigh the litigation risk with counsel and Tribal Council. Two paths:

Apply for TAS over undisputed areas only
Affirm the disputed areas are still claimed but excluded for now → then continue to Question 2.
Or — decline TAS
Still productive: use inherent Tribal authority; obtain §103 (investigative) and §105 (programmatic, via a Performance Partnership Grant) grants; run monitoring, environmental education, indoor-air, and radon programs — no TAS required.
▼ If proceeding with TAS, continue to Question 2 ▼
Question 2 · Sources & location Are there emission sources — and where are they?
MANY sources on the reservation

Regulatory TAS — become the primary implementing authority (TIP, permitting, inspections, enforcement). Choose one of two forms:

Develop your own programs → EPA approval
You tailor the rules and become the primary enforcer; once approved they are federally enforceable.
Take delegation of federal rules
No rule-writing — issue permits & inspect, but all enforcement actions are referred to EPA.

Applicable sections:

§110 TIPO₃ / PM / SO₂·NO₂·Pb / CO plansPSD/NSR §§164·165·167·173Title V (§§501–507)§111 NSPS§112 toxics§114§129§303
FEW sources on the reservation

A targeted regulatory TAS may still fit — e.g., a wood-smoke burning ordinance.

§110 TIPPM NAAQS
Outside sources affect you / you want a voice

Participatory “good-neighbor” TAS — get early review of, and standing to comment on, others’ SIPs and permits.

§126(a)/(b)§110(a)(2)(D)(i)§505(a)§105 grants§319 monitoring§107(d) designations
What changed: the draft routes “undisputed = Yes” to weigh the risks and “disputed = No” to move forward — reversed. It also never connects Q1 to Q2, leaves the “many sources?” sub-questions with no answer branches, and buries the good-neighbor question (which applies to every Tribe) under “few sources,” duplicating its list. Regulatory and participatory TAS are not mutually exclusive — a Tribe can pursue both.

03 Findings by theme

Decision-tree logic

HighQuestion 1’s Yes/No branches are inverted
The “undisputed = Yes” branch triggers risk-weighing and disputed-area carve-outs — content that only makes sense when boundaries are disputed — while “No” routes to a confident “move forward.” The Yes branch even contradicts itself, asserting no dispute yet repeatedly discussing “the disputed area.”
Fix: rephrase to a non-negated question (“Are any boundaries disputed?”) and swap the branch contents.
HighQ1 and Q2 are never connected
No Q1 outcome says “proceed to Question 2,” and Q2 opens with no antecedent — so the tree reads as two disconnected fragments, even though Q2 is the mechanism for the “which sections” choice Q1 alludes to.
HighSub-questions with no answer branches
“Are there many sources?” and “no or few sources?” are posed but carry no Yes/No outcomes — the guidance that follows applies regardless of the answer, so the questions drive no decision.
MedNot MECE — the “few sources” case has no home
“Few” satisfies “Yes, there are sources” at the top but the Yes branch only anticipates “many,” pushing it into the No branch — so it straddles both answers.
MedOutside-sources question misplaced & duplicated
It applies to every Tribe but is nested under “no/few own sources,” and its six-section list is repeated verbatim from the Yes branch — a drift hazard that has already begun.
ObsNo reservation vs. non-reservation branch
The criteria require different jurisdictional showings (map + legal description vs. a statement of legal counsel), but Q1 collapses them into one question.

Regulatory accuracy

High“Land base in jeopardy” overstates the risk
A TAS determination fixes the scope of CAA regulatory jurisdiction, not land title or ownership. As written it could be read to mean a Tribe risks losing land by applying.
Fix: say an adverse ruling could narrow recognized CAA regulatory jurisdiction.
HighRegional haze mis-cited as “169(a)(1)”
§169 is PSD definitions. The visibility/regional-haze authority is §169A (and §169B).
HighCriminal-enforcement footnote is legally inaccurate
Tribes generally lack criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians (Oliphant), and an MOU cannot confer it — EPA/DOJ retains that authority. This footnote needs legal review.
Med“500 – Title V” — no such section
Title V is §§501–507 (program authority §502). The Part 70 / Part 71 sub-detail is correct.
Med§126 description garbled & imprecise
Conflates the 126(b) petition authority with “early review”; distinguish 126(a) source notice from 126(b) petitions.
Med“Program Partnership Group (PPG)” is wrong
It’s a Performance Partnership Grant. Also note §105 grants obtained without TAS carry the standard 40% match — the 5%/10% match is a TAS benefit.
Low§129 mislabeled & imprecise plan ranges
§129 is solid-waste combustion — landfills fall under §111/§112. Ozone is §§181–185; SO₂/NO₂/Pb is §§191–192 (§193 is a savings clause); use “NO₂,” not “NOx.”
LowCitation format & terms of art
Use “40 CFR part XX” (not “CFR 40”), “appropriate governmental entities,” and one term (“affected State”) for the Title V review concept.

Structure & redundancy

HighEligibility criteria stated twice
Full with citations in Section B, then again in an abbreviated, re-ordered list that demotes and strips the boundaries criterion — the one both the Risk section and Q1 turn on. State them once and cross-reference.
MedGood-neighbor block duplicated verbatim
Define it once as a named “Participatory TAS” package and reference it from both branches.
MedOrdering front-loads benefits, buries risk
A dozen upbeat benefit bullets up front; risk is one thin paragraph much later, and the “Why apply” purpose statement arrives after the benefits it should frame.
MedInconsistent voice
Sections A/B use “you/your”; the Why, Risk, and tree sections switch to third person — a sign of stitched-together sources.

Clarity & editing

HighScrambled “good neighbor” sentence
“Prevention of Significant Deterioration Permits” is split across two sentences, with “Deterioration Permits.” orphaned two sentences downstream — and the defect is duplicated verbatim in the second block.
HighGarbled thesis sentence
TAS is a tool not just for protecting… as well as Tribal Sovereignty — sets up “not just” but never delivers “but also.”
MedSentence dies mid-clause
The decision tool provides examples for addressing these criteria based on — cut off at the exact hinge between the criteria and the tree.
MedPervasive possessive errors & undefined “AGE”
“the Tribes jurisdictional boundaries,” “under the Tribes authority” (want Tribe’s); and the acronym AGE is used without ever being defined.

Usability

HighNo flowchart
A document named “Decision Tree” delivers prose nested three-to-four levels deep — leadership and Council cannot trace which outcome attaches to which answer. (See section 02 above.)
High~25 bare CAA section numbers
Terse labels, no plain-language “what this does for you,” all meaning deferred to an external link. A reader can’t tell from “164 – Class I redesignation” whether it applies to them.
Med“Weigh the pros and cons” isn’t actually supported
No side-by-side comparison; benefits get a dozen bullets, risk one paragraph naming essentially one hazard — nothing on cost, staffing, ongoing obligations, or timeline.
MedNo next steps or glossary
No application checklist, EPA contact, timeline, or templates; several acronyms (NSR, PM, CO, SO₂, MOU) are never expanded.

Cross-cutting gaps

HighEligibility conflated with operational authority
TAS eligibility is a threshold status; a Tribe must then separately submit each program for EPA approval before implementing or enforcing. State the two-stage sequence explicitly.
HighThe capability criterion gets no branch
The barrier most likely to stop a Tribe — technical/administrative capability — has no decision node, self-assessment, or route to the “plan to gain expertise” option the document itself mentions.
HighThe risk of not pursuing TAS is never stated
So the mandated pro/con weighing is structurally impossible; the “no TAS” path is framed as cost-free rather than a trade-off (loss of decision-making voice, dependence on state/EPA).
MedCitizen-suit / ongoing-oversight exposure omitted
Section A itself notes an approved program is enforceable “by the EPA and citizens” (§304) — a real ongoing obligation absent from the risk discussion.
ObsNo “not legal advice” disclaimer; no mention of consortia
The document makes many legal characterizations without a disclaimer, and never mentions multi-Tribe/consortium applications — a path suited to the capacity-constrained Tribes it aims to reassure.

04 What to keep

  • The eligibility backbone. Section B, the §105 match figures, §49.7(a)(8), the PSD/NSR cluster and Part 70/71 distinction are all accurately cited — the model for fixing the weaker section list.
  • Scenario-based section mapping. Translating situations (many / few / off-reservation sources; regulatory vs. participatory; own-program vs. delegation) into the right CAA sections is what earns the “tool” label.
  • Sections A and B prose. Clean, parallel, second-person — the register the whole document should match.
  • The “decline TAS” branch. Fully developed with concrete alternatives; shows a “no” is not “nothing.”
  • Repeated direction to consult counsel, leadership, and Council.

05 Prioritized recommendations

  1. Fix Question 1’s inverted logic.
    Reword to “Are any boundaries disputed?” Put risk-weighing and the partial-inclusion strategy under disputed, confident section-selection under undisputed, and add a partial-dispute path. Highest-stakes defect — it can misdirect a legal decision.
  2. Build a real, accessible flowchart as the centerpiece (see section 02).
    Two decision points, every edge labeled, connectors joining Q1 into Q2, a small set of labeled outcome boxes. Pair any image with a text equivalent and alt text (Section 508).
  3. Replace bare section dumps with tables.
    A “goal → section → plain-sentence effect → regulatory/participatory” table, plus a two-column benefits-vs-risks table. De-duplicate the good-neighbor list into one named package.
  4. Correct the regulatory errors.
    §169A for regional haze; Title V as §§501–507; legally review the criminal-enforcement footnote; “Performance Partnership Grant”; §129 as solid-waste combustion; tighten plan ranges and citation format.
  5. Rewrite the risk section for a real trade-off.
    Correct “land base in jeopardy”; add the risk of not applying; add cost/staffing/timeline/enforcement/citizen-suit considerations; caveat the “undisputed areas only” strategy.
  6. Close the eligibility/authority and capability gaps.
    State the two-step sequence; add a capability node pointing to phased programs, delegation, consortia, and the “plan to gain expertise”; finish the dangling bridge sentence.
  7. Consolidate structure.
    State the four criteria once; reorder to Background → Why → Benefits → Risks → Criteria → Tree; add a “Next steps” section (checklist, EPA contact, timeline, templates); reconcile the “affected state” vs. §505(a) overlap.
  8. Full copyedit & finish the draft.
    Repair the split “Prevention of Significant Deterioration Permits” sentence (both copies) and the “not just… but also” thesis; standardize possessives, voice, and acronyms; add a glossary and a real title; add a “not legal advice” disclaimer.
